What Is an LLM and How Can It Work Inside Your Business Operation?

Large Language Models power the AI tools you already use — from ChatGPT to code assistants. Understanding their strengths and limits helps you decide where to deploy them for real business impact.

March 17, 2026 · 6 min read

You've probably used an LLM today without thinking about it. Asked ChatGPT a question. Used an AI assistant to draft an email. Let autocomplete finish a sentence in Google Docs. Behind all of these is the same type of technology: a Large Language Model.

But here's what most business leaders get wrong: they think of LLMs as "the thing that answers questions." That's like thinking of electricity as "the thing that turns on lights." It does that, yes — but the real value is in the thousand other things you can power with it once you understand what it actually is.

What an LLM Actually Is

A Large Language Model is an AI system trained on enormous amounts of text — books, websites, code, documents, conversations. Through that training, it develops an understanding of how language works: not just grammar, but meaning, context, intent, and structure.

The crucial insight: LLMs don't retrieve stored answers. They generate new ones. Every response is assembled in real time based on patterns the model learned during training. That's what makes them flexible enough to write an email, summarize a legal document, extract data from a messy spreadsheet, and debug a piece of code — all without being specifically programmed for any of those tasks.

What LLMs Are Genuinely Good At

After deploying LLMs across dozens of business operations, we've found they consistently deliver value in five areas:

  • Text generation: Drafting emails, proposals, summaries, and reports. Not perfect first drafts, but solid 80% drafts that a human can polish in minutes instead of writing from scratch in an hour.
  • Information extraction: Reading unstructured documents — contracts, invoices, support tickets, product reviews — and pulling out the specific data points you need. This alone saves some teams 10+ hours per week.
  • Classification: Sorting incoming messages by intent, urgency, or category. A support inbox that used to require a human to triage every message can be pre-sorted with 90%+ accuracy.
  • Question answering: Give the model your internal documentation, and it can answer employee questions about policies, procedures, or product details — in natural language, instantly, 24/7.
  • Translation and adaptation: Not just language translation, but adapting content for different audiences, tones, or formats. Turn a technical spec into a client-friendly summary. Turn meeting notes into action items.

What LLMs Are Not Good At (And When to Use Something Else)

Knowing the limits is as important as knowing the strengths:

Precise math: LLMs are language models, not calculators. For anything involving exact calculations, use actual computation tools. LLMs can write the formula — they shouldn't execute it.

Real-time data: LLMs work with the information you give them. They don't browse the internet in real time (unless connected to a search tool). If your workflow needs live stock prices or current inventory levels, the LLM needs a data feed.

Consistency at scale: Ask the same question ten times, you might get ten slightly different answers. For processes that require identical outputs every time, traditional automation is more reliable.

Domain expertise without context: An LLM can sound authoritative about topics it doesn't truly "understand." Always provide relevant context and verify critical outputs — especially in legal, medical, or financial domains.

How Companies Are Actually Using Them

The businesses getting the most value from LLMs aren't using them for flashy demos. They're embedding them into specific, high-volume operational workflows:

  • A logistics company uses an LLM to read incoming shipping emails, extract order numbers, dates, and addresses, and populate their tracking system automatically
  • A law firm uses an LLM to summarize contracts and flag clauses that differ from their standard templates
  • A SaaS company uses an LLM to draft personalized onboarding emails based on each customer's industry, company size, and stated goals
  • An HR department uses an LLM to answer employee questions about benefits, PTO policies, and expense procedures from an internal knowledge base

The Practical Starting Point

Don't start with "how can we use AI?" Start with "where do our people spend time on language-heavy work that follows a pattern?" That's where LLMs fit.

The answer is usually hiding in plain sight: in the inbox, in the support queue, in the document review process, in the weekly report that someone spends three hours writing even though it follows the same structure every time.

LLMs are not magic. They're a tool — a remarkably flexible one — that works best when applied to a well-understood problem. Map the process, define the inputs and outputs, and let the model handle the language-heavy middle.
